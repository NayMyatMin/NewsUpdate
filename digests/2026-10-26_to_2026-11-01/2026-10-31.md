According to a paper published by Google, its AI-powered security agent has
uncovered more than 500 XSS vulnerabilities
across its web applications since entering full operation in January. Google PageBreak uses Gemini models to autonomously investigate potential vulnerabilities, but reports them only after deterministic validators successfully reproduce the exploit against a running application, resulting in a near-zero false-positive rate.
With PageBreak, every vulnerability hypothesis is tested against a running environment with a real exploit, such as executing JavaScript for XSS, writing to a world-readable location for path traversal, or triggering outbound DNS for RCE.
Michał Bentkowski
, information security engineer at Google, explains:

Rather than simply hypothesizing bugs based on code patterns, the system closes the loop by verifying potential flaws against running environments. This approach results in a near-zero false positive rate, ensuring that we avoid overloading product teams with unverified vulnerability reports.

Abstract:
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being used as cognitive interfaces that shape how users interpret evidence, communicate, and make decisions. As deployed systems become more multimodal, tool-using, and multi-agent, safety failures no longer arise only from isolated harmful outputs, but also from how agents update, store, and propagate epistemic commitments across retrieval, memory, tools, and peer communication. In this paper, we define belief as an engineering abstraction for evidence-based commitments that guide downstream action. Building on this view, we propose the Explicit Belief Multi-Agent System (EB-MAS), which externalizes belief states into a structured substrate, uses belief operators to propose candidate updates, and applies a Cognitive Firewall to bound or reject updates that are weakly evidenced but strongly driven by vulnerability cues. We also discuss the risk of group belief at the group level, including manufactured consensus, collusion, and cascading misinformation, and outline practical integration challenges around grounding, false positives, scalability, and ethical misuse. Therefore, scalable agent safety requires not only better output filtering or stronger base models, but also explicit interfaces for tracing, calibrating, and constraining belief revision at runtime.

Abstract:
Frontier large language models write correct CUDA kernels for most single-operator benchmark tasks, yet their accuracy collapses on accelerators whose architecture and programming language they have rarely seen. We present a case study of an LLM agent loop built for such accelerators. Our example is the FuriosaAI RNGD NPU, programmed in a recent, sparsely documented Rust DSL, where the loop optimized three Gemma-4-12B decode kernels over 12 days on a shared, scarce device. The loop builds its own target knowledge: it writes and checks a summary of the DSL documentation, measures the chip with microbenchmarks, records compiler-derived legality rules, and revisits this knowledge when the toolchain changes, rechecking rules and measurements where it can. A role-based search over per-kernel Design Trees explores whole-kernel designs and accumulates small accepted changes in the best version of each kernel. Because device time is scarce and readings are noisy, cheap checks screen new designs before any device run, and late in the run a change was accepted only after repeated comparisons with the best kernel on the device. The final kernels reach a 9.293x geomean speedup over the official baseline, each within 1.25x of the cycles needed just to move its weights.
